STEP 17 — Orders Database

Our flow will eventually be:

                 PRODUCTS
                    ↓
                  CART
                    ↓
                CHECKOUT
                    ↓
              ┌───────────┐
              │  ORDERS   │
              └───────────┘
                    ↓
             ORDER_ITEMS
                    ↓
          Reduce Product Stock
                    ↓
              Clear Cart


Checkout must use a PostgreSQL transaction

We'll use:

pool.connect()
      ↓
BEGIN
      ↓
Get cart
      ↓
Get cart items + products
      ↓
Validate stock
      ↓
Create order
      ↓
Create order_items
      ↓
Reduce stock
      ↓
Clear cart
      ↓
COMMIT
      ↓
Release connection

One thing we'll also handle

We'll calculate the order total on the server from the database, rather than trusting a total sent from React:

React
  ↓
"Checkout"
  ↓
Backend gets cart
  ↓
Backend calculates:
price × quantity
  ↓
Backend creates order

So a client cannot simply send:

{
  "total": 1
}

and purchase ₹1,00,000 worth of products for ₹1. 



🔥 Understand the most important part

The entire checkout is inside:
BEGIN
   ↓
Get cart
   ↓
Get cart items
   ↓
Check stock
   ↓
Calculate total
   ↓
Create order
   ↓
Create order items
   ↓
Reduce stock
   ↓
Clear cart
   ↓
COMMIT

If anything fails:

BEGIN
   ↓
Something fails ❌
   ↓
ROLLBACK
   ↓
Database returns to previous state

That's why we use:

const client =
    await pool.connect();

instead of:

pool.query(...)

for the whole checkout operation.

🔐 Why FOR UPDATE?

This line is particularly important:

FOR UPDATE OF p

Imagine stock is:

iPhone 15
stock = 5

Two customers try to buy the last 5 units at almost exactly the same time.

The transaction locks the relevant product rows:

Customer A
    ↓
locks product row
    ↓
checks stock
    ↓
updates stock

Customer B has to wait for the lock.

This helps prevent race conditions during checkout.

💰 Another important point

Notice that the client never sends:

{
    "totalAmount": 69999
}

Instead:

Database
   ↓
Product price
   ↓
Cart quantity
   ↓
price × quantity
   ↓
Backend calculates total

💡 Why multiply by 100?

Razorpay expects the amount in the smallest currency unit.

Therefore:

₹139,998
      × 100

13,999,800 paise

Math.round(

STEP 19.7 — Payment Verification

Now we need to handle the most important security part:

Razorpay Payment
       ↓
payment_id
       ↓
signature
       ↓
Backend verifies signature
       ↓
✅ Valid
       ↓
payment_status = Paid

We must not simply trust this from the frontend:

{
  "paymentId": "...",
  "success": true
}

Instead, Razorpay gives us a cryptographic signature, and our backend verifies it using:

RAZORPAY_KEY_SECRET
1. Add signature verification

In paymentController.ts, add:

import crypto from "crypto";

              ↓
       Create Razorpay Order

       Verify Payment

razorpay_order_id
razorpay_payment_id

The signature must be generated by Razorpay.
